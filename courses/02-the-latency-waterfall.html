The latency waterfall: where the second goes

"Make it faster" is not a plan. A plan names the stage. Tonight Callwell's six-stage call flow from session 1 becomes a waterfall: every stage a bar, laid out in time from the caller's last word to the first syllable they hear, with the stages that run in parallel drawn in parallel. The page computes it live from dated vendor targets, names the largest stage on the path, and lets you break it: add a tool call, switch streaming off, cut the silence wait. One of those levers makes the number better and the calls worse, and the page measures how much worse on real conversation.

Part 1 · covers what "fast enough" means

How fast is fast enough? Four answers

4 min live

There is no agreed number. There is a human baseline from conversation research and three published vendor or practitioner targets, and they are far apart. Pick one on purpose and write down why, because the bench compares everything against it.

LiveThe human baseline, and what it does not mean3 min▶

Stivers and colleagues (PNAS, 2009) timed responses to yes/no questions in ten languages, from Japanese to Danish. The overall mode was 0 ms, the median +100 ms and the mean +208 ms; the fastest language mean was Japanese at +7 ms and the slowest Danish at +469 ms. Levinson and Torreira (2015) add the puzzle: gaps are "of the order of 200 ms" yet producing even a single word takes "around 600 ms and up". People hit 200 ms only by planning their reply while the other person is still talking.

So "humans respond in 200 ms" is true as an average for one kind of turn, and misleading as a target. No pipeline that waits for silence before starting can reach it, and the data are question-answer pairs, not a booking conversation. Use it to explain why 1.5 seconds feels slow, not as a specification.

LiveThe vendor and practitioner targets3 min▶

800 ms: Kwindla Hultman Kramer's widely shared advice (GitHub gist, June 2025) is to aim for "800ms median voice-to-voice latency (eventually)", with 1,500 ms acceptable in a first proof of concept. Under one second: LiveKit's docs say conversations "feel natural when end-to-end response latency stays under one second". 1,115 ms: Twilio's cascaded latency guide (November 2025) sets a mouth-to-ear median target of 1,115 ms with an upper limit of 1,400, and calls its numbers "starting benchmarks, not best-in-class".

Read the budgets behind them and they do not add up to their own headlines. Kramer's component budget (network 200, turn detection and transcription 400, model 500, voice 200) sums to 1,300 ms. Twilio's components (speech to text 350, model first token 375, voice 100) sum to 825 before network. The 800 ms builds get there by overlapping stages, which is what the bench shows.

Self-studyTwo claims to drop2 min▶

"500 ms is the magic number." No primary source was found for it on 2026-11-08; it circulates in vendor marketing. If you use it, attribute it to whoever said it and call it an opinion.

"Callers hang up after N seconds." No primary source was found for any version of this. Twilio's guide explicitly makes no such claim. Measure your own abandonment instead (session 5).

Real world

Twilio budgets the trip as well as the models. Twilio's guide separates the platform turn gap (target 885 ms, measured where its servers can see) from the mouth-to-ear turn gap (1,115 ms), and its diagram puts about 40 ms between the caller's device and the media edge plus buffering and decoding on top. The phone network is a stage you pay for whichever model you choose.

Part 2 · covers the critical path, preemption and the tool call

The wait, the overlap and the tool

4 min live

A waterfall is not a sum. Stages that can start at the same moment run in parallel, and only the longest chain, the critical path, decides when the caller hears anything. Shortening a stage off that path changes nothing. That is why "use a faster model" is often the wrong first move.

LivePreemptive generation: start thinking before the turn is over3 min▶

LiveKit's field guide (April 2026) says "Preemptive generation is enabled by default" in LiveKit Agents: the model starts "as soon as a partial transcript of the user's speech is available", while the turn detector is still deciding. On the bench that is why the model bar starts at 100 ms. Untick it and the model waits for the turn to close and the final transcript: the total goes from 800 to 1,175 ms, 375 ms over target, with the wait still the largest stage.

The honest catch, in LiveKit's own words: if the final transcript differs and the reply has to be regenerated, "latency will not be improved". The bench assumes the guess was right. It is the machine version of what Levinson and Torreira describe in people: plan the reply while the other person is still talking.

LiveThe break button: one tool call3 min▶

Press "Add a tool call" at defaults. The model's first output is now a request to Callwell's booking system, which waits for the confirmed turn (you do not book on a guess), takes a modelled 400 ms, and then the model runs again for another 375 ms before the voice can start. The verdict reads 1,575 ms to first audio, 775 ms over the 800 ms target. Nothing about the tool call streams, and nothing can overlap it.

Two fixes, neither on the bench: say something while the tool runs, which OpenAI's guide says to measure separately from the real answer, or make the tool faster and call it earlier. Timing each tool as its own span is how you find out which; tracing per stage is taught in learn-ai-observability, cost and latency per span.

Self-studyStreaming off, and the reply-length trap2 min▶

Untick streaming and every stage waits for the whole output of the one before: speech recognition starts after the wait, the model writes the whole 40-token reply (20 ms per token, modelled), the voice synthesises all of it (10 ms per token, modelled), and only then does audio leave. The total is 2,725 ms and the largest stage is no longer the wait: it is "Rest of the reply" at 800 ms. Without streaming, a longer answer is a slower answer; with it, length barely touches the first syllable.

Part 3 · covers the anti-lever and the pipeline comparison

The trap: cut the wait

4 min live

The wait is the largest stage, so the obvious move is to shorten it. Slide the threshold down and the total falls. What the total does not show is the caller who paused for breath in the middle of their date of birth and got interrupted. The bench measures that second number on 24 real two-person conversations where the speakers could not see each other, the nearest open analogue of a phone call.

LiveWhat the cut-in number measures3 min▶

The sample is 24 dialogues from the HCRC Map Task Corpus (CC BY 4.0 per its download page), recorded in the condition where the two speakers could not see each other. Every silence is classified by who spoke either side: a pause when the same person carried on, a gap when the other person took the turn. There are 3,520 pauses, 3,014 gaps and 1,218 overlapping handovers. A pure silence timer set to T would have started talking in every pause of T or longer, while the speaker had not finished. At 500 ms that is 1,149 pauses, 27.2 per 100 speaker changes; at 200 ms it is 55.2.

The limits, said out loud: these are people giving map directions, not callers booking a clinic; nobody in the sample was talking to a machine; and a real turn detector also listens to words and pitch, not just silence (session 3). Treat the numbers as the cost of a pure timer, measured on real speech, not as Callwell's future complaint rate.

LiveSpeech-to-speech on the same bench2 min▶

Switch the pipeline to speech-to-speech. The model's first audio is a modelled 375 ms (no vendor publishes the figure; it is set equal to the cascade's model budget so the comparison isolates the hand-offs). The total is 1,075 ms, 275 ms over target: it saves the separate voice stage, but the model cannot start until the turn is confirmed, so it cannot hide behind the wait the way the cascade's preemptive model does. Cut its threshold to 200 ms and it reaches 775 ms, with the same 55.2 cut-ins.

That is not a ranking. LiveKit rates realtime models fastest, and with a quicker first audio they would be. The point is that the label decides nothing; the inputs and the overlap do.

Real world

Researchers measured the timer problem in 2010. Heldner and Edlund analysed three corpora, including this one, and found that pauses and gaps have similar length distributions: in HCRC Map Task a 500 ms threshold caught 47.5% of the gaps and 56.0% of the pauses. Our segmentation gives lower levels, 35.4% of gaps and 32.6% of pauses at 500 ms, because we used the corpus's word timings rather than their own speech detection. The conclusion is the same in both: a silence timer cannot tell a thinking pause from a finished turn.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 2 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · At Callwell's defaults, a vendor offers a model with a 250 ms first token instead of 375 ms. What happens to the 800 ms total?

At defaults the model finishes at 475 ms and the turn closes at 600 ms. A faster model finishes earlier and waits longer. Only a change to a stage on the critical path (uplink, wait, voice, downlink) moves the total.

2 · Cutting the silence threshold from 300 ms to 200 ms on the bench changes the total by how much, and the false cut-ins by how much?

Below 375 ms the model's first token, not the wait, is on the critical path, so the total stays at 675 ms. The cut-in count is measured on real pauses and keeps climbing as the threshold falls. That is the anti-lever: a better latency number bought with worse calls, and below 375 ms not even the latency improves.

3 · Which statement about latency targets is supported by the sources?

No primary source was found for A or B. The human mean is real (Stivers et al. 2009) but people reach it by planning while the other person talks, which no silence-waiting pipeline can do. Pick a target, name its source, and say why.
