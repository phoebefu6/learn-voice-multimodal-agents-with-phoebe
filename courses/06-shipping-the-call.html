Part 1 · covers telephony at concept level

How a call reaches your agent

5 min live

A caller dials a number on the public phone network (the PSTN). A carrier such as Twilio owns that number and turns the call into something your software can touch. From there it takes one of two routes. On the first, the carrier streams the raw call audio to your server over a WebSocket and you run the speech stack. On the second, the carrier hands the call over SIP straight to a realtime model, and your server only answers a webhook. The question that decides between them is the one from session 1: what do you need to see and control?

LiveRoute A: a media stream you run the speech stack on2 min▶

Twilio Media Streams "stream raw audio from live phone calls over WebSockets to your application in near real-time" (Twilio docs, modified 2026-10-30, read 2026-11-08). A bidirectional stream, opened with <Connect><Stream>, lets your application send audio back into the call. The format is fixed: the encoding is always audio/x-mulaw, the sample rate always 8000, one channel. Audio you send back must be the same, base64 encoded, with no audio file header bytes in the payload.

This is the route for Callwell's cascaded pipeline. You choose the speech recogniser, the model and the voice, you keep a transcript at every hand-off, and you own the plumbing: decoding the frames, resampling if a stage wants a different rate (OpenAI's Realtime API accepts G.711 mu-law directly; PCM input is 24 kHz), turn detection, barge-in and, on a WebSocket, truncating the reply the caller did not hear (session 3).

LiveRoute B: SIP straight to a realtime model2 min▶

Twilio's tutorial (2025-08-29, a vendor tutorial) creates an Elastic SIP Trunk and sets its address to sip:<project-id>@sip.api.openai.com;transport=tls. When a call arrives, OpenAI fires a realtime.call.incoming webhook at your project, and in the tutorial's words "the first thing you have to do is respond and say you are accepting the call". The realtime model then hears and speaks; your server never touches an audio frame.

Treat the detail as vendor-tutorial tier. OpenAI's own SIP guide returned 404 when we read on 2026-11-08, and a secondary summary of it says the app can also reject, refer (transfer) or hang up a call by REST. That transfer is the handoff in Part 2, so confirm how it works on the current guide before you design around it.

Self-studyWhat concept level means here, and what is left out3 min▶

Concept level means you can say which route a call takes, what format the audio is in at each point, and which code is yours. It does not mean you can run a telephony stack. Left out on purpose: SIP signalling, codecs beyond naming G.711, number porting, carrier contracts and regional phone rules. A carrier's own documentation and support team own those.

The routes map onto session 1's choice. Route A suits a cascade, or a realtime model you still want to front with your own server for logging. Route B suits a speech-to-speech build where the realtime session owning the audio is the point. Either way you still need the turn settings from session 3 and the eval gates from session 5.

Real world

The carrier's docs set your audio format, not your model's. Twilio's WebSocket message reference (modified 2026-10-08, read 2026-11-08) says of the media format: "Value is always audio/x-mulaw". Whatever your speech stack prefers, 8 kHz mu-law is what arrives and what you must send back. On route B the same constraint exists; the realtime service handles it for you, which is exactly why you cannot see it.

Part 2 · covers failure handling

Fallbacks and the warm handoff

5 min live

A booking agent that never hands a call to a person will, sooner or later, keep an upset caller in a loop. Session 1's map already marked the door to a human; session 4 added the unreadable critical field. Tonight that door becomes a rule checked on every turn, and the handoff becomes warm: a summary travels with the transfer, so the receptionist starts from what is already known and the caller never says their date of birth twice.

LiveThe triggers: when the agent stops2 min▶

Callwell's triggers come straight from earlier sessions. The caller asks for a person, at any point, in any words: never argue, never ask why. The caller sounds distressed. A medical question: the agent books appointments and does not interpret symptoms or letters. Recognition fails twice on the same field, such as a clinician's name that keeps coming back wrong. A critical field is unreadable on the referral photo and the caller cannot supply it (session 4's routing rule). A tool is down, so the booking cannot be checked.

Two notes. Distress is the hardest trigger to detect from a transcript, which is one reason session 5 asked for a sample of real calls to be reviewed by a person. And a silent caller is not yet a trigger: OpenAI's server_vad has an optional idle_timeout_ms (5,000 to 30,000 ms, no default listed, read 2026-11-08), described as a timeout "after which a model response will be triggered automatically", which lets the agent ask "are you still there?" before anyone transfers anything.

LiveThe handoff summary: what travels with the transfer2 min▶

A cold transfer drops the caller into a queue and the receptionist asks "how can I help?", so the caller starts again. A warm transfer passes a short summary first: who the caller is and which identity checks are done, what they want, what is already confirmed and what is still open, why the agent handed over, and any file the caller sent. The receptionist reads it before saying hello.

Keep it short enough to read in the seconds before pickup, write it from confirmed fields only (session 4's status flags make that easy), and never put a guess in it as a fact. If the agent was unsure of a name, the summary says "clinician not confirmed", not its best guess.

Self-studyDegraded modes when a vendor is down3 min▶

Plan one fallback per stage, before launch, and test each one. In a cascade, each stage is a separate vendor you can swap: a second speech recogniser behind a flag, a second voice, a smaller model for the acknowledgement while the main one recovers. In a speech-to-speech build, LiveKit's pipeline page (read 2026-11-08) names the cost plainly: "Less provider flexibility: You're committed to a single provider's model for the full speech-to-speech path." If that provider fails, the whole speech path fails at once, and the fallback is a route around the agent, not a swap inside it.

Whatever the pipeline, the last fallback is the same: the call goes to a person, or to a message that says plainly the line is busy and when someone will call back. A tool outage is its own case: the agent can still take the details and promise a call back, as long as it never confirms a booking it could not check.

Real world

Two vendor pages, one design rule. LiveKit lists "Less provider flexibility" as a realtime model's cost, which is a fallback problem as much as a pricing one. OpenAI's voice agents guide (read 2026-11-08) tells builders to measure an acknowledgement such as "I'm checking" separately from the answer the caller needs. Put together: when something is slow or down, say something true and short at once, then either recover or hand over. Silence is the one response that is never a fallback.

Part 3 · covers recording and AI notices, and cost

Consent, notices and cost per minute

5 min live

This part is general and sourced, and it is not legal advice. What a clinic must tell callers, and what it may record, is decided by its lawyers for its jurisdictions. What a builder can do is know the shape of the rules, put the notices in the greeting, keep recordings under the same rules as any other patient data, and understand which meters run during a call. Prices are vendor list prices read on 2026-11-08; re-verify before you rely on them.

LiveConsent and notices, in general terms2 min▶

Recording. The Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press recording guide (read 2026-11-08, no page date, a journalism reference) sets out the US shape: one-party consent is the federal floor, about 11 states primarily require all-party consent, and on calls that cross state lines the guide advises assuming the stricter state law applies. State rules change; whoever advises the clinic checks each one.

Notices. As practice, not as a legal requirement we are stating: tell callers in the greeting that they are talking to an automated assistant, that the call is recorded or transcribed, and how to reach a person. It costs a few seconds and removes the most common reason a caller feels misled.

Health context. Recorded or transcribed calls are data, and your privacy rules and contracts cover them like any other patient record: who can hear them, how long they are kept, which vendors process them. On HIPAA specifically, secondary summaries report that HHS guidance treats audio-only telehealth as allowed with reasonable safeguards and treats VoIP and stored audio as electronic; the HHS page itself could not be read on 2026-11-08 (reported tier). Read the regulator's guidance at hhs.gov, with whoever advises the clinic.

LiveOutbound against inbound: what FCC 24-17 covers2 min▶

The FCC's Declaratory Ruling FCC 24-17 (adopted 2024-02-02, released 2024-02-08, read at source) holds that the TCPA's restrictions on "artificial or prerecorded voice" cover current AI technologies that generate human voices, so such calls need the prior express consent of the called party, absent an emergency purpose or an exemption.

The ruling is about calls you place. Callwell's agent answers calls that patients place to the clinic, which is a different situation. The moment Callwell adds outbound reminder calls in the agent's voice, those calls fall under the ruling, and the consent question has to be answered before the first one dials. Scope creep from inbound to outbound is the common way a team walks into this without noticing.

Self-studyCost per minute: the formula and the dated prices4 min▶

The formula, for a cascade: cost per call minute = speech-to-text minutes + model tokens + speech characters + telephony minutes, each divided back to one minute of call. For speech-to-speech, swap the first three for audio tokens in and out plus any text tokens. The figure above is the reason it is a formula and not a number: the meters run at different times, so the same agent costs different amounts on a chatty call and a quiet one.

Real world

A 2024 ruling and a journalism guide, both read at source. FCC 24-17 settled that an AI-generated voice is an "artificial" voice under the TCPA, which put outbound AI calls under the consent rules that already applied to robocalls. The RCFP guide's advice on calls between states is to "assume that the stricter state law will apply". Neither is written for clinic booking lines, which is exactly why the greeting carries notices and the outbound question goes to whoever advises the clinic.

★ Step 3 · triggers and summaryHand to a person when ANY of: - caller asks for a person (any wording, no questions asked) - caller sounds distressed - caller asks a medical question - recognition fails twice on the same field - a critical referral field is unreadable and caller cannot supply it - booking tool or a speech vendor is down Summary (confirmed fields only, never a guess): who: name, date of birth [confirmed / not confirmed] wants: new booking | change | cancel, site, clinician confirmed: list of fields the caller confirmed by voice still open: list of fields not yet settled why now: which trigger fired files: referral photo file id + extracted fields with status

Make the launch decision and write the week-one watch list. The gate itself runs as in the launch-gate seam; what a voice agent adds is the list of numbers someone looks at every day of the first week, each with the target set in an earlier session.

★ Step 6 · launch decision and week oneDecision: go / hold / roll back (gates 1-7 all ticked, or hold) Rollback: carrier forwards the number back to the front desk; who can trigger it: [named owner], how fast: [minutes] Watch daily in week one, against the targets you set: - first-syllable latency, p50 and p95 (budget from session 2) - false cut-ins per call (threshold from session 3) - task success: booking right end to end (eval gates, session 5) - handoff rate, and the reasons it fired (triggers, step 3) Review a sample of real calls with a person, under the clinic's data rules (consent and retention settled first).

A rising handoff rate is not always bad news. In week one it often means the triggers are doing their job on calls the eval set never imagined. Read the reasons before you tune them down: a cluster of "recognition failed twice" on one clinician's name is a speech fix, while a cluster of medical questions is a sign the greeting should say what the line is for.

Homework

Before you go live

30 min
  1. Draw your route. Route A or B for your product, with the formats at each point and the coral box of code you own. If you cannot fill the box, you are not ready to pick the route.
  2. Run a warm handoff by hand. Give a colleague only your summary format, filled in from a test call, and ask them to pick up the call. Note every question they had to ask the caller again; each one is a missing field.
  3. Switch one stage off. In a test environment, disable one vendor and call in. Write down what the caller heard and how long before it happened. Fix whatever made silence the answer.
  4. Fill in your own cost line. Replace every modelled input in step 5 with a number from your own test calls and current price pages, dated. Keep the labels.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 6 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Which route hands your server raw 8 kHz mu-law call audio over a WebSocket?

Twilio Media Streams send raw call audio over a WebSocket, and the format is always audio/x-mulaw at 8000 Hz, one channel. On the SIP route the realtime model handles the audio and your server only gets the webhook. 24 kHz PCM is a format the OpenAI Realtime API takes as input, not what the phone network delivers.

2 · What does the FCC's Declaratory Ruling FCC 24-17 cover?

The ruling is about outbound calls under the TCPA. Callwell's agent answers calls patients place, so it is a different situation, but any outbound reminder calls in the agent's voice would be covered. Recording consent and HIPAA are separate questions, sourced elsewhere and still not legal advice.

3 · Why does a warm handoff pass a summary along with the call?

The summary carries who, what they want, what is confirmed, what is open and why the agent handed over. It holds confirmed fields only, never guesses, which is why D is wrong; B and C are not what a summary is for.
