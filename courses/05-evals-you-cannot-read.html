Learn Voice and Multimodal Agents with Phoebe · Session 5 of 6

Evals for a conversation you cannot read

A text agent leaves you a transcript that is the conversation. A voice agent leaves you audio, and the transcript is only one model's guess at it. The caller who was talked over, the four seconds of dead air while a tool ran, the date of birth heard as the wrong day: none of it is visible in a tidy log. Tonight we decide what to measure on a call before we look at the words, compute word error rate by hand and watch it rank the wrong line as worst, and build a harness of simulated callers that goes through the same phone path real ones do. Then we write Callwell's eval sheet.

Part 1 · covers what to measure on a voice call

Measure the call before the words

5 min live

Eval basics do not change on a phone line: you still need a golden set of scenarios with known right answers, and you may still use a model as a judge. Both are taught in learn-ai-evals, golden sets and LLM-as-judge, and agent-level evals in learn-ai-agents, agent evals. Nothing here repeats them. What voice adds is a stack of measures at three heights, and the habit of reading it from the top down.

LiveTask success first2 min▶

Task success is the one measure the clinic cares about: at the end of the call, was a booking made, and is every field of it correct? A call that books Doctor Lindqvist at Northgate when the caller asked for Doctor Okafor at Riverside is a failure, however fluent it sounded. Score it per scenario, from the booking system's record, not from the transcript.

Under it sits slot accuracy, scored per critical field: clinician, date of birth, site, day and time. A per-field score tells you where calls fail, which a single success rate cannot. Next to both sits appropriate handoff: when the caller asked for a person, sounded distressed or asked a medical question, did the agent transfer them, with a summary? A handoff that should have happened and did not is a failure even if a booking was made.

Only then do you look down the stack. The lower layers explain a failure; they do not define one.

LiveLatency and interruption measures2 min▶

Latency, per stage and per call. OpenAI's voice agents guide (read 2026-11-08) says to "measure how long callers wait for a useful spoken answer", to compare the median and the 95th percentile across similar calls, and to measure an acknowledgement such as "I'm checking" separately from the answer the caller actually needs. A build whose median is fine and whose 95th percentile is three times worse has a tool call or a cold start you have not found yet. LiveKit's metrics name the pieces you will log: end-to-end latency, time to first token from the model, time to first byte from the voice. Spans and per-span timing are taught in learn-ai-observability, cost and latency per span; the waterfall you built in session 2 is the shape those spans should take.

Interruptions, three ways. From session 3: a false cut-in is the agent starting to talk while the caller was only pausing; a missed barge-in is the caller talking over the agent and the agent carrying on; talk-over is the total time both voices are on the line at once. All three are timing facts in the audio, and none of them appears in a transcript.

Self-studyWhat to log on every call4 min▶

If you cannot replay a call, you cannot evaluate it. For each call, keep one record that links:

  • the call id, scenario id (for test calls) and pipeline version: model, voice, turn settings;
  • both audio channels, caller and agent, kept separate so overlap can be measured;
  • the transcript per turn with timestamps, and the agent's reply text before it was spoken;
  • a trace with one span per stage per turn: end-of-turn wait, transcription, model first token, tool calls, voice first byte;
  • turn events: turn start, turn end, barge-in, truncation, and what the agent heard versus what it said;
  • the outcome from the booking system, the fields written, and whether a handoff happened and why.

Recorded calls and transcripts are data that your privacy rules and contracts cover; session 6 points to where that is decided. Keep test calls and real calls apart from day one.

Real world

OpenAI separates the "I'm checking" from the answer. In its voice agents guide (read 2026-11-08), OpenAI tells builders to measure acknowledgements separately from the useful answer. The reason is the Callwell tool call from session 2: a quick "one moment" can make the first syllable fast while the caller still waits for the slot. Log both, gate on the second.

Part 2 · covers word error rate

Word error rate, computed

7 min live

Word error rate (WER) is the standard score for speech recognition. Line up what was said (the reference) against what the recogniser wrote (the hypothesis), word by word, with the fewest edits. Count substitutions S, deletions D and insertions I. Then WER = (S + D + I) / N, where N is the number of words in the reference. Because insertions are counted and N is not, WER can exceed 100%. Every error weighs the same (Wikipedia, reference tier; NIST's sclite scoring tool is reported to use equal weights by default). That last property is the one that matters on a booking line.

Now run it on all six constructed Callwell lines. The widget below aligns each pair live, using a stated normaliser, and says whether the booking survived.

Read it in this order. Start with the overall line: 7 errors in 56 reference words, WER 12.5%, and bookings broken 3 of 6. A 12.5% recogniser sounds usable; half the bookings did not survive it. Then find the highest WER in the table: c3, "Riverside" written as "river side", 22.2%, and the booking is right, because "river side" still resolves to the Riverside site. Then c2: "fourteenth" heard as "fourth", only 8.3%, and the booking is broken, because a date of birth that is wrong by ten days is a different patient record. Then c6: the caller's "Um" dropped, also 8.3%, and harmless. Same score as c2, opposite consequence. Last, the try box: it opens at 16.7% on "fourteenth" against "fourth" in a six-word line. Type two of your own lines into it.

LiveHow alignment counts2 min▶

The alignment is an edit distance over words (Levenshtein): find the smallest number of substitutions, deletions and insertions that turns the reference into the hypothesis. A substitution is a reference word replaced by a different word (okafor became oak). A deletion is a reference word with nothing against it (c4's final "Tuesday", c6's "Um"). An insertion is a hypothesis word with nothing against it (offer, side).

Ties happen: "riverside" against "river side" could be counted as substitute-then-insert or insert-then-substitute. The widget breaks ties the same way every time (prefer a match or substitution, then a deletion, then an insertion), so its numbers are repeatable. The total is the same either way; which word gets which label can differ between tools.

Because N counts only the reference, a recogniser that hallucinates a long sentence over a two-word answer can score well above 100%. That is not a bug in the formula; it is what the formula is for.

LiveWhy WER misleads on a booking line2 min▶

WER weighs every word the same. A booking does not. On Callwell's six lines, the ranking by WER and the ranking by harm disagree: the worst-scoring line (c3, 22.2%) did no harm, and two of the three broken bookings sit at 8.3% (c2) and 16.7% (c1). c4, at 25.0%, broke the booking by dropping the one word that corrected the day.

So WER stays on the eval sheet as a diagnostic: when slot accuracy drops, WER on the failing lines tells you whether the recogniser is the cause. It does not go on as a launch gate. The gate is slot accuracy on the fields that matter and task success on the call.

Two more cautions. Phone audio reaches your server at 8 kHz (session 1), so any WER measured on clean, wideband audio is an upper bound on how well the same recogniser does on Callwell's line. And these six lines are constructed to show error types; they say nothing about any real recogniser's rate.

Self-studyNormalisation rules, and vendor numbers4 min▶

Before alignment, both lines are normalised. The widget lower-cases, keeps a-z, 0-9 and apostrophes, and turns everything else into a space. That is why c5, which differs only in punctuation, scores 0.0%. Change one rule and the number moves: strip apostrophes and "I'd" becomes "id"; write numbers as digits in one line and words in the other and "nineteen eighty two" against "1982" becomes three substitutions or worse; decide whether "um" counts and c6 changes. Write your normaliser down next to every WER you report.

This is why vendor WER figures are not comparable with each other: different test sets, different normalisers. learn-ai-media teaches that in full, with measured examples, in how it fails, measured; we do not repeat it.

One dated example of what a vendor number looks like. Deepgram's Nova-3 launch post (2025-02-12) reports a median streaming WER of 6.84% against 14.92% for an unnamed competitor, on Deepgram's own test set. It is vendor-reported, on audio that is not your callers', and the same post states the relative improvement as 54.2% in one place and 54.3% in another. Read as of 2026-11-08; re-verify before you rely on it, and measure on your own recorded lines either way.

Real world

A vendor's headline WER is measured on the vendor's audio. Deepgram's Nova-3 post (2025-02-12) is explicit that its 6.84% median streaming WER comes from its own test set, and it names no competitor. That is normal practice, not a trick. It means the number you should trust for Callwell is the one you compute on your own phone-quality lines, with your normaliser printed beside it.

Part 3 · covers audio against transcript judging, and simulated callers

Judging what you cannot read

5 min live

A model judge that reads transcripts is the cheapest way to grade hundreds of calls, and it is blind to exactly the things that make a voice agent feel broken. A judge that listens to audio hears more, but is newer and less tested. And to have calls to judge before launch, you need callers: simulated ones, played through the same phone path a real caller takes. No single primary source sets out a standard method for any of this; what follows is current practice, labelled as practice, with its limits drawn in.

LiveTranscript judge or audio judge2 min▶

A transcript judge is a language model reading the call's text and grading it against a rubric: did the agent confirm the date, stay inside policy, offer a person when asked. It is cheap, repeatable and well understood. It cannot hear tone, so a reply read in a flat voice to an upset caller scores the same as a kind one. It cannot see talk-over, because two people speaking at once become two tidy lines. And it cannot see dead air, because four seconds of silence leave no text at all.

An audio judge listens to the recording and can, in principle, hear all three. In practice it is newer and less calibrated: there is no primary source we read that sets out how well audio judges agree with people, so treat their scores as a signal to check, not a verdict. The robust pattern today is to split the work: a transcript judge for content, plain timing code over the two audio channels for talk-over and dead air (these are measurements, not judgements), and a slot checker against the booking system for the outcome.

LiveBuilding personas2 min▶

A persona is a scripted caller with a goal, some facts and a behaviour. Start with the four that break voice agents most, each built from what sessions 2 and 3 taught you to fear:

  • The mid-sentence pauser: "My date of birth is the fourteenth of..." then a long pause, then "March". Tests the end-of-turn settings from session 3.
  • The interrupter: talks over the slot offer with "no, not Thursday". Tests barge-in and truncation.
  • The caller on a noisy line: the same script with background noise mixed in before the codec. Tests the recogniser where it is weakest.
  • The one who asks for a person: "Can I just speak to someone?" on the second turn. Tests the handoff.

The persona's lines are spoken by a synthetic voice, then pushed through an 8 kHz phone codec before they reach your agent, so the agent hears what it would hear on a real call. Skip the codec and you are testing a cleaner line than you will ship. Record both channels, and run each persona more than once: the agent's replies vary, so one pass proves little.

Self-studyCalibrating a judge, and the limits of simulation4 min▶

Any model judge, transcript or audio, needs calibrating against people before you trust it: have two people grade a sample of calls, measure how often the judge agrees with them and with each other, and fix the rubric where they disagree. The method is taught in learn-ai-evals, LLM-as-judge. For voice, add one step: have the people listen, not read, so the judge is calibrated against what callers hear.

Simulated callers have a hard limit, drawn as the coral gap in the figure. Synthetic voices are cleaner than real ones, scripted callers are more patient, and nobody writes a persona for the caller who puts the phone down to shout at a child. A simulated pass tells you the agent handles the cases you thought of. It does not tell you about the ones you did not. So a simulated pass is necessary, not sufficient: plan to review a sample of real calls after launch, with consent and data handling settled first (session 6).

Real world

LiveKit names the timings; nobody names the judge. LiveKit's documentation (read 2026-11-08) names the latency metrics a voice eval can log per turn: end-to-end latency, time to first token, time to first byte. We found no vendor or paper, read at source, that sets out a calibrated method for judging voice calls from audio. That gap is the honest state of the field on 2026-11-08: measure the timings, check the slots, and treat any audio judge as a new instrument that still needs calibrating.

Build-along

Callwell's eval sheet

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Write the sheet that decides whether Callwell's (constructed) agent may answer real callers. Use Callwell, or swap in your own product and keep Callwell as the worked answer. Every threshold below is constructed for teaching: your numbers will differ, and they should come from your own clinic, your own callers and your own risk appetite.

Write 20 golden scenarios as a table. One row per scenario: who calls, what they want, what the right outcome is. The mix below is constructed to cover Callwell's main paths and the side doors; weight yours by what your real line receives.

★ Step 1 · 20 golden scenarios (constructed)id type caller wants right outcome G01 ordinary booking Doctor Okafor, Riverside, next Tuesday booked, all fields correct ... (8 ordinary bookings, G01-G08: vary clinician, site, day) G09 reschedule move Thursday to Tuesday, same doctor old slot freed, new booked ... (4 reschedules, G09-G12: include one "no, not Thursday") G13 photo letter book from a texted referral letter referral reason read back ... (3 photo letters, G13-G15: one blurred, one rotated) G16 handoff "Can I speak to someone?" warm transfer, summary ... (3 handoff cases, G16-G18: one distressed, one medical question) G19 noisy line ordinary booking, street noise booked, or handoff after two failed reads G20 noisy line reschedule, poor signal same as above

Define task success and slot accuracy. In words a colleague could score without asking you. Decide which fields are critical, and score them from the booking record, never from the transcript.

★ Step 2 · the two top measuresTask success (per scenario): PASS if the booking record matches the scenario's right outcome on every critical field, OR the scenario expects a handoff and a warm transfer happened with a summary. Anything else is FAIL. Slot accuracy (per field, across all scenarios that use it): critical fields: clinician, date of birth, site, day, time correct = booking record value == scenario value, exactly "river side" resolving to Riverside counts as correct; "fourth" for "fourteenth" does not

Compute WER in the try box on two of your own lines. Type a line you would say to your agent as the reference, and a plausible mishearing as the hypothesis. Write down S, D, I, N and the WER the widget prints, and whether the mishearing would break your booking. Your numbers will differ from anyone else's in the room.

★ Step 3 · two lines of your ownLine A reference: ______________________________ hypothesis: ______________________________ S __ D __ I __ N __ WER ____% booking broken? yes / no Line B reference: ______________________________ hypothesis: ______________________________ S __ D __ I __ N __ WER ____% booking broken? yes / no Lesson to write under them: which line had the higher WER, and was it the one that broke the booking?

Set the latency gates from session 2. Gate the first syllable at both p50 and p95, measured from traces on the golden scenarios, and log acknowledgements separately from the useful answer. Kwindla Hultman Kramer's 2025 guidance aims for 800 ms median and calls 1,500 ms acceptable for a proof of concept (practitioner, read 2026-11-08); the p95 gate below is a constructed choice built from those two figures.

★ Step 4 · latency gates (constructed thresholds)Measure: caller's last word -> first audio of the agent, per turn Source: traces, one span per stage (session 2 waterfall) Gate: p50 under 800 ms AND p95 under 1,500 ms Separate: time to the useful answer on tool-call turns (acknowledgement "one moment" logged on its own line) Re-verify the 800 / 1,500 figures before you rely on them; your own callers decide the real gate

Add the interruption measures from session 3. These come from the two audio channels and the turn events, not from text. Run the pauser and the interrupter personas several times each.

★ Step 5 · interruption measuresfalse cut-in agent starts speaking while caller is mid-pause (pauser persona; count per call) missed barge-in caller speaks over agent for more than a moment and the agent keeps talking (interrupter persona) talk-over total seconds both channels carry speech at once truncation after a barge-in, does the agent's context hold only what the caller actually heard? (session 3) Gate (constructed): at most 1 false cut-in across 20 scenarios, 0 missed barge-ins

Write the gate: what blocks launch. The launch gate itself, who signs it and how it is reviewed, is taught in learn-agent-launch, the launch gate. Here you only write the voice lines that feed it.

★ Step 6 · the launch gate linesBLOCKS LAUNCH (any one): task success below 18 of 20 golden scenarios any critical field below 100% slot accuracy first-syllable p50 at or over 800 ms, or p95 at or over 1,500 ms more than 1 false cut-in, or any missed barge-in any handoff scenario not transferred DOES NOT BLOCK, BUT IS LOGGED: WER per line (diagnostic: explains slot failures) transcript-judge and audio-judge scores (until calibrated) AFTER LAUNCH: review a sample of real calls; simulated passes are necessary, not sufficient (All thresholds constructed for Callwell.)

Put WER under the table, not in it. The moment WER becomes a gate, people tune for it: a recogniser change that drops WER while breaking dates of birth will pass. Keep the gate on what the caller and the clinic feel, and keep WER where it is useful, as the first number you look at when a slot starts failing.

Homework

Before session 6

30 min
  1. Finish your 20 scenarios. Complete the table from step 1 for your own agent, with the right outcome written for every row. Weight the mix by the calls your line actually gets.
  2. Find your c3 and your c2. Write one line where a recognition error would score high on WER and do no harm, and one where a small error would break the task. These two lines are the best argument you will have for gating on slots.
  3. Write two personas. Pick the two behaviours from Part 3 your callers are most likely to show, and write each as a short script with a goal, facts and a behaviour. Note how you will route them through an 8 kHz codec.
  4. List what you log. Against the "what to log on every call" card, mark what your build records today and what it does not. Bring the gaps; session 6 decides what you may keep.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 5 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · In the WER widget's six constructed Callwell pairs, which line has the highest WER yet still books correctly?

c3 scores 22.2% (one substitution and one insertion over 9 reference words), the highest in the widget, and "river side" still resolves to the Riverside site. c2 is only 8.3% and breaks the booking; c1 at 16.7% breaks it too; c6 is harmless but scores 8.3%. WER ranks the lines differently from the harm they do.

2 · Word error rate can exceed 100%. Why?

WER = (S + D + I) / N with N the number of reference words. Insertions add to the top without adding to the bottom, so a recogniser that writes a long sentence over a two-word answer can go past 100%. Every error weighs the same, which is why B is wrong too.

3 · Why does a judge that reads only transcripts miss talk-over?

Talk-over is a timing fact: both channels carry speech at the same moment. A transcript records who said what, in order, and drops when. Measure talk-over with timing code over the two audio channels; use the transcript judge for content.
