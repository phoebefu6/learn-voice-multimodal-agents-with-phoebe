Learn Voice and Multimodal Agents with Phoebe · Session 3 of 6

Turn-taking: when to speak, when to stop

A text agent knows the user has finished: they pressed send. A voice agent has to guess, from silence and from the words, many times a call. Guess too early and it talks over a caller who was only thinking. Guess too late and every reply carries dead air. And when the caller talks over the agent, it has to stop, and remember only the part of its sentence the caller actually heard. Tonight we take apart the three mechanisms that decide all of that: the voice activity detector, the end-of-turn decision, and barge-in with truncation. Then we tune Callwell's settings one moment of the call at a time.

Part 1 · covers voice activity detection

Hearing speech: voice activity detection

5 min live

A voice activity detector (VAD) answers one narrow question, over and over on short frames of audio: is someone speaking in this frame? On top of that yes or no it runs a small state machine. Enough speech in a row and it switches to "speaking"; enough silence in a row and it switches back and fires "speech stopped". It does not know what was said. It does not know whether the sentence is finished. It only knows how long the quiet has lasted.

LiveWhat a VAD hears, and what it does not3 min▶

What it hears. Energy and voice-like sound, frame by frame, scored against a threshold. OpenAI's Realtime API reference (read 2026-11-08) gives the server_vad defaults: threshold "defaults to 0.5" on a 0 to 1 scale, prefix_padding_ms "Defaults to 300ms" (audio kept from just before speech was detected, so the first syllable is not clipped), and silence_duration_ms "Defaults to 500ms", the quiet it needs before it fires speech stopped. Pipecat's Silero VAD (read 2026-11-08) has the same shape with different numbers: confidence 0.7, start_secs 0.2 (speech must run that long before the state flips to speaking), stop_secs 0.2 (silence before it flips back to quiet), min_volume 0.6.

What it does not hear. Meaning. "The fourteenth of" and "that's all, thanks" followed by the same silence look identical to a VAD. A cough over a quiet line can trip it; a soft speaker on a noisy phone can fall under the threshold. Raising the threshold cuts false starts from background noise and misses quiet callers; lowering it does the reverse. These are vendor defaults dated 2026-11-08: re-verify before you rely on them.

Self-studyTwo stop_secs that mean different things3 min▶

Pipecat has two settings called stop_secs, and mixing them up is an easy way to break a build. On the Silero VAD, stop_secs is 0.2 s: "Time in seconds of silence required before transitioning back to QUIET state". On Smart Turn, Pipecat's turn model, stop_secs is 3.0 s: the silence after which the turn ends anyway, even if the model said the caller was not finished ("the turn is automatically marked as complete").

They work together. The VAD flips to quiet quickly, after 0.2 s, which gives the turn model an early look at the words. The turn model decides; the 3.0 s fallback stops a caller who drifts off from leaving the line open forever. Pipecat's own docs recommend keeping the VAD at 0.2 s when Smart Turn is on: "We recommend 0.2 seconds, which is the default value."

One more trap from a neighbouring layer: Deepgram's endpointing "is enabled by default and set to 10 milliseconds". That finalises a transcript (it sets speech_final to true); it is not a decision that the caller's turn is over. Treat a transcript endpoint and a turn decision as two different signals.

Real world

OpenAI states the scope in one line. The Realtime VAD guide (read 2026-11-08) says create_response and interrupt_response "are only used in speech-to-speech conversations", and that "In transcription sessions, VAD only controls how audio is chunked." The same detector either chops audio into pieces for a transcriber or decides when the model speaks, depending on the session. Know which job yours is doing before you tune it.

Part 2 · covers end-of-turn detection

Is the turn over? Timers against meaning

6 min live

The VAD says the caller went quiet. The end-of-turn decision says whether they have finished. The simplest version is a timer: if the silence lasts T milliseconds, the turn is over. The trouble is in the data. In real conversation, a pause inside one person's turn and a gap before the other person speaks have almost the same length, so any T that catches the gaps also fires on the pauses. The smarter version reads the words, and waits longer when the sentence is plainly unfinished.

LiveWhy a timer cannot win: pauses and gaps look alike3 min▶

Heldner and Edlund (2010, Journal of Phonetics 38) measured pauses (silence inside one speaker's turn), gaps (silence before the other speaker starts) and overlaps in three corpora. On the HCRC Map Task, a 500 ms threshold catches 47.5% of gaps and 56.0% of pauses: the timer fires on more of the silences where the speaker was not finished than on the ones where they were. Their verdict: "turn-taking is generally less precise than is often claimed."

We measured the same corpus ourselves: 24 HCRC Map Task dialogues, two people who could not see each other (the nearest open analogue of a phone call, but not phone calls). The median gap is 335 ms and the median pause is 323 ms, twelve milliseconds apart. At 500 ms, 32.6% of pauses and 35.4% of gaps are that long, which works out to 27.2 false cut-ins per 100 speaker changes. Our levels are lower than Heldner and Edlund's because we segment by timed words where they used their own speech activity detection; the shape is the same in both. Teach both numbers, and the reason they differ.

LiveWhat semantic detection buys, and what we cannot measure here3 min▶

OpenAI semantic_vad. Instead of a silence length you set eagerness: "low will let the user take their time to speak", "high will chunk the audio as soon as possible". The API reference gives the ceilings: "low, medium, and high have max timeouts of 8s, 4s, and 2s respectively", and "auto is the default and is equivalent to medium".

LiveKit Agents documents five turn detection modes: its turn detector model (the default), the realtime model's own detection, VAD only, STT endpointing, and manual. Endpointing is fixed by default, with a minimum and maximum delay; the JS reference gives minDelay 500 ms and maxDelay 3,000 ms (read at source), and the Python defaults of 0.5 s and 3.0 s are reported from a code snippet, not read at source. Pipecat makes Smart Turn v3 its default user turn stop strategy: it runs locally on ONNX, supports 23 languages, and Pipecat reports inference "under 100ms" on low-cost cloud instances (vendor-reported).

The honest limit. The widget below measures timers only. Testing a semantic detector needs the words and the model, not just silence lengths, and no vendor publishes a false cut-in rate for its turn model on open data. What semantic detection should buy you is fewer cut-ins on unfinished sentences and faster replies on finished ones; on your own calls, that is a claim to measure in session 5, not to assume. All names and defaults dated 2026-11-08: re-verify before you rely on them.

Real world

Heldner and Edlund (2010) checked the folk number. Across three corpora, including the Swedish Map Task and the HCRC Map Task, gaps cluster around 200 ms, overlaps make up about 40% of between-speaker intervals, and 70 to 82% of intervals are under 500 ms. A 500 ms threshold caught 51.1% of gaps and 59.6% of pauses in the Swedish Map Task, and 47.5% and 56.0% in HCRC. There is no primary source for "500 ms is the magic number"; there is one showing that at 500 ms a timer cannot tell the two apart.

Now drag it yourself. Read it in this order: the threshold, then false cut-ins, then the two shares (pauses the timer fires on, real gaps still running), then the wait it adds to every reply, then the two curves lying almost on top of each other.

Part 3 · covers interruptions

Barge-in, truncation and backchannels

5 min live

The other half of turn-taking runs the opposite way: the agent is talking and the caller starts. That is barge-in, and on a phone line it is normal, not rude: people correct, agree, and get ahead of a list. Stopping the audio is the easy part. The part builders miss is the model's memory. Unless you tell it otherwise, the model believes it said its whole sentence, including the half the caller never heard.

LiveWhy truncation matters2 min▶

In OpenAI's Realtime API, interrupt_response and create_response both default to true, and both apply only in speech-to-speech conversations. When the caller barges in, "the server cancels it and sends response.done with response.status set to cancelled". The audio stops. But the model generated the whole sentence, and it is sitting in the conversation as if it had been said.

conversation.item.truncate fixes that. You send the item, the audio content index, and "that played duration as audio_end_ms", and the server removes "the unplayed portion of the model's last response from the conversation", including its transcript. At Callwell (constructed) that is the difference between the next turn saying "Great, Tuesday at nine" and the model later asking "So was that Thursday at two?" about an offer the caller never heard.

LiveBackchannels are not interruptions2 min▶

Callers say "mm-hmm", "yeah", "right" while the agent talks. That is a listener signalling "keep going", and an agent that stops dead on every "mm-hmm" is exhausting to call. LiveKit Agents (read 2026-11-08) gives you two filters on what counts as an interruption: min_duration and min_words, "Minimum number of words to be considered as an interruption." Set min_words to 2 (our constructed choice) and a lone "yeah" no longer stops the agent; "no, Thursday" still does.

LiveKit also resumes after a false interruption by default: "the agent resumes speaking from where it left off after a false interruption." The docs show a 2.0 s timeout as an example; that it is the default is reported only, not read at source. Pick your own thresholds and measure them.

Self-studyThe WebSocket chore2 min▶

Whether truncation happens for you depends on the transport. OpenAI's conversations guide: over WebRTC and SIP, "The server will automatically truncate unplayed audio when there's a user interruption." Over a WebSocket, the server does not know how much of its audio your client actually played, because the buffering is on your side. So your client has to do two things the moment the caller's speech is detected: stop playback (flush whatever audio is queued), and send conversation.item.truncate with the milliseconds it really played.

Get the second number wrong in either direction and the context lies: too long and the model thinks the caller heard words they did not; too short and it repeats itself. Phone calls bridged through a WebSocket media stream (session 6) are exactly this case.

Real world

Pipecat treats an interruption as a priority signal. Pipecat's pipeline guide (read 2026-11-08) lists an interruption frame for "Interruption control" and marks system frames, such as the one saying the user started speaking, as high priority: "interruptions do not discard them." The architecture says it plainly: when the caller speaks, everything queued to be said is disposable, and the fact that they spoke is not.

★ Step 5 · interruption policy (constructed)Counts as barge-in: 2 or more words (LiveKit: min_words); a lone "mm-hmm" or "yeah" is a backchannel, keep talking On barge-in: stop playback at once, cancel the response Truncate: transport is a WebSocket, so OUR client sends conversation.item.truncate with audio_end_ms = ms played Never: confirm a slot the caller did not hear in full

Write what you will measure in session 5. The widget's numbers came from two people reading maps. Your callers are not them, so these settings are hypotheses until your own calls say otherwise.

★ Step 6 · what session 5 will measureFalse cut-ins: agent started while the caller was mid-turn, per 100 turns, split by moment (date of birth first) Missed barge-ins: caller spoke 2+ words, agent kept talking Wasted waits: caller had finished, agent sat out the ceiling Context drift: model referred to words that were truncated Your numbers will differ from the widget's; that is the point.

Switch before you ask. The turn setting that matters is the one live when the caller answers, so send the update just before the agent asks the question, not after the answer starts. And keep a ceiling on every patient setting: low eagerness still ends at 8 s, so a caller who wanders off is never left on a silent line.

Homework

Before session 4

30 min
  1. Find your settings. Look up the turn detection and VAD settings your framework is actually running with today, not the ones you think you set. Write each one down with the date you read it.
  2. List your recall moments. Every question in your call where the caller has to remember something (a date, a number, a name). Those are your low-eagerness candidates.
  3. Test your truncation. Interrupt your own agent mid-list, then ask it "what did you just offer me?" If it names an option you did not hear, truncation is not working.
  4. Say "mm-hmm" at it. Three times, during three different replies. Count how often it stops. Decide whether that count is acceptable for your callers.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 3 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Why can a silence threshold alone not separate a caller who is thinking from a caller who has finished?

On 24 HCRC Map Task dialogues the median pause is 323 ms and the median gap 335 ms; at 500 ms the timer fires on 32.6% of pauses while 35.4% of gaps are still running. Heldner and Edlund (2010) found the same shape with different levels. The VAD hears the silence fine; it cannot know what the silence means.

2 · The caller barges in halfway through "I have Tuesday at nine or Thursday at two". What does sending conversation.item.truncate with audio_end_ms achieve?

Stopping playback is the client's job, and cancelling is what barge-in already does (response.done with status cancelled). Truncation fixes the memory: OpenAI describes it as removing "the unplayed portion of the model's last response from the conversation". Over WebRTC and SIP it happens automatically; over a WebSocket your client sends it.

3 · You set OpenAI semantic_vad eagerness to "low" for the date-of-birth question. What does that mean?

The API reference (read 2026-11-08): "low, medium, and high have max timeouts of 8s, 4s, and 2s respectively", and low "will let the user take their time to speak". It is patient, with a ceiling. Interruption is a separate setting, interrupt_response.
