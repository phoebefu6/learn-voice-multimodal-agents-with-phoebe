Learn Voice and Multimodal Agents with Phoebe · Session 1 of 6

The voice pipeline: cascaded or speech-to-speech

You have shipped a text agent. It reads a message, thinks, calls a tool, writes a reply, and nobody minds if that takes four seconds, because the user is looking at a typing indicator. Put the same agent on a phone line and three things arrive that text never had: a clock the caller can hear, a question about whose turn it is, and a person who cannot scroll back to reread what you said. Tonight we draw the two ways to wire a voice onto an agent, a chain of three models or one model that hears and speaks, and see what each costs in latency, control and observability. Then we map one booking line end to end.

Part 0

Where we start

The running case is a constructed Callwell example. Callwell is an invented clinic group with three sites, Riverside, Northgate and Harbour, and one phone number for bookings. Today a receptionist answers it. The clinic wants an agent on that line: callers say what they need, some text a photo of a referral letter from their doctor, everyone expects an answer about as fast as a person would give one, and some of them interrupt. The text chatbot on Callwell's website already books appointments through the same booking system. Everything in the six sessions is a decision you will face when you put your own agent on a call.

Part 1 · covers what the voice channel adds to an agent

Same agent, new channel

4 min live

The agent loop does not change when the channel does: the model still reads context, decides, calls tools and writes an answer, and your guardrails still apply. The loop, tools and memory are taught in learn-ai-agents, the agent loop, and nothing here repeats them. What changes is everything around the loop: something has to listen, decide the caller has finished, speak, and stop speaking the moment the caller cuts in.

LiveThree things text never had3 min▶

A clock the caller can hear. In a chat window, silence is a typing indicator. On a call, silence is dead air, and people start talking again into it. Conversation research puts the typical gap between one person finishing and the next starting at around 200 milliseconds (Stivers et al. 2009 report a mean of +208 ms across ten languages). No agent hits that; session 2 measures how far off a real build is and where the time goes.

A question about whose turn it is. Text has a send button. Speech has pauses, and a pause in the middle of "my date of birth is the fourteenth of... March" looks exactly like a finished sentence to a microphone. Session 3 is entirely about this.

A listener who cannot scroll. A three-paragraph answer with a bulleted list is fine on a screen and unusable in an ear. Replies have to be short, ordered and confirmable, which changes how you prompt the same agent.

Self-studyPhone audio is not studio audio3 min▶

A phone call reaches your server as narrowband audio. Twilio's Media Streams documentation states the format plainly: the encoding is always audio/x-mulaw, the sample rate is always 8000, one channel. That is G.711 mu-law, the format of the public phone network for decades. A browser microphone gives you far more: the OpenAI Realtime API, for instance, takes PCM at 24 kHz mono, and also accepts G.711 mu-law and A-law directly so a phone stream does not need resampling.

Two consequences for Callwell. First, speech recognition accuracy on a phone line is lower than on a headset, so any accuracy figure measured on clean audio is an upper bound. Second, the format conversion is a stage: if you resample, it is one more thing in the timing trace. Session 6 covers how the call reaches your server at all.

Real world

OpenAI's own advice is about the wait, not the words. OpenAI's voice agents guide (read 2026-11-08) tells builders to "measure how long callers wait for a useful spoken answer", to compare the median and the 95th percentile across similar calls, and to measure an acknowledgement such as "I'm checking" separately from the answer the caller actually needs. That is a latency discipline text agents rarely need, and it is the first thing a voice agent gets judged on.

Part 2 · covers cascaded against speech-to-speech

Two pipelines, drawn as mechanisms

6 min live

There are two ways to wire the listen and speak stages. A cascaded pipeline (OpenAI calls it chained) strings three models together: speech recognition turns audio into text, a language model turns that text into a reply, and speech synthesis turns the reply into audio. A speech-to-speech model, sold as a realtime model, takes audio in and gives audio out from one model. The difference that matters is what you can see at each hand-off.

LiveCascaded: three models you can each see and swap3 min▶

LiveKit's documentation calls the cascaded pipeline "the right default" for most production agents, and names what it costs: "Each stage adds latency", and because speech recognition produces text, "prosody and emotional cues in the user's speech don't reach the LLM". A caller who says "fine" through gritted teeth reaches the model as the word fine.

What it buys is visibility. OpenAI's voice agents guide recommends the chained path "when you want to inspect or transform text between speech recognition" and the reply, and when "each stage needs to be visible or replaceable". At Callwell that matters twice: the transcript is a record of what the caller asked for, and the reply text can be checked against the booking before a word of it is spoken. You can also change the speech recogniser without touching the agent.

LiveSpeech-to-speech: one model, fewer hand-offs, less to read3 min▶

A realtime model "consumes and produces speech directly, in a single model" (LiveKit). The gain LiveKit names is "Lower end-to-end latency: Combining stages into one model removes the inter-stage hand-offs", and it hears tone, laughter and hesitation that a transcript drops. OpenAI's guide describes the same path as one model that interprets audio, decides what to do and responds in speech, with the session handling turns, tools and interruptions.

The costs LiveKit lists are the ones a clinic feels: "Delayed transcripts: Realtime models don't produce interim transcripts", "Less provider flexibility" because one provider owns the whole speech path, and "Harder to audit: Without a text trail at every stage, debugging and compliance review take more work." Session 2 puts numbers on the latency claim, and finds it depends on the inputs more than the label.

Self-studyThe hybrid, and the frameworks that wire either one3 min▶

LiveKit documents a third shape: a realtime model for understanding, paired with a separate text-to-speech voice, "to keep realtime speech understanding and add scripted speech". It is the answer when you want the model's ear but need a specific voice or exact wording for, say, a legal notice.

You rarely wire any of this by hand. LiveKit Agents and Pipecat are open-source frameworks that run either pipeline. Pipecat models everything as frames flowing through processors; its system frames, which carry events such as "the user started speaking", are high priority and "interruptions do not discard them". The DeepLearning.AI short course "Building AI Voice Agents for Production", made with LiveKit and RealAvatar, walks the same architecture in seven video lessons, one of them on optimising latency. Watch it for the code; the certificate stays theirs.

Real world

Two vendors, two framings, one trade. LiveKit's comparison table rates cascaded end-to-end latency "Moderate" and both realtime options "Fastest", and still recommends cascaded as the default. OpenAI sells the realtime model and still documents the chained path for teams that need to inspect the text. Neither is hedging: they are telling you the decision is about control and audit, with latency as the tiebreaker you can engineer.

Part 3 · covers streaming at every stage

Streaming decides most of the second

5 min live

Whichever pipeline you pick, the single biggest latency decision is whether each stage streams: starts handing on its output before it has finished. A recogniser that streams has most of the transcript before the caller stops. A language model that streams hands its first words to the voice while it is still writing the rest. A voice that streams starts playing the first syllable while it synthesises the second sentence. Without streaming, every stage waits for the whole output of the one before.

LiveWhat streams at each stage, and what it saves3 min▶

Speech recognition streams partial transcripts while the caller is still talking, so the final transcript arrives soon after they stop instead of after a full pass over the recording. The language model streams tokens, so the voice can start on the first clause. Speech synthesis streams audio chunks; ElevenLabs' latency guide lists "Leverage streaming" among its four principles. The network streams too: audio goes up and down in small frames over WebRTC or a WebSocket, never as a file.

The session 2 bench computes the difference on Callwell's defaults: 800 ms to the first syllable with every stage streaming, 2,725 ms with nothing streaming. Those are inputs added up, not a measurement of any product, and your numbers will differ; the gap is the point.

Decide on control first, latency second. If you must keep a record of what was said, check replies before speaking them, or swap vendors, start cascaded. If the conversation's feel is the product and the audit need is light, try speech-to-speech. Then measure, because the next session shows the latency winner flips with the inputs.

★ Step 6 · decision recordWe put Callwell's booking agent on a cascaded pipeline with streaming at every stage, a text-message photo path and a warm handoff, because the clinic needs a checkable transcript and reply. Count the arrows. On Callwell's map there are twelve. Each one is a place where time is spent and something can be lost: audio quality on the phone network, a word on the way to text, a field on the way out of a photo. When a call goes wrong in production, the first question is which arrow it went wrong on, and the map is how you will answer it.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 1 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Callwell must keep a record of what each caller asked for and check every reply against the booking system before it is spoken. Which pipeline fits first?

The need is a text trail and a checkpoint before speech, which is exactly what the cascaded hand-offs give you. LiveKit lists "Harder to audit" and delayed transcripts as realtime costs. Speed is not "always" on either side: session 2 shows the latency winner depends on the inputs.

2 · On Callwell's defaults, what does turning streaming off at every stage do to the time before the caller hears a reply?

The work is the same; the overlap disappears. With streaming, transcribing and thinking run while the end-of-turn wait is still counting; without it they queue. Both numbers are the session 2 bench's computed totals on modelled inputs, not a product measurement.

3 · Which statement about speech-to-speech models is supported by the vendor documentation read for this session?

LiveKit's pipeline page names both: lower latency from removing hand-offs, and "Harder to audit: Without a text trail at every stage". A, B and C describe the cascade's properties or its costs, the wrong way round.
